Supporting question 01

How does AI automation affect existing jobs and employment opportunities?

We start with the work itself: which occupations already show up in real AI use, how exposure varies by country, and whether women and men face the same risk.

On a small screen, swipe a chart sideways to see all of it.

Which jobs are most exposed to AI?

This chart ranks professions by how much of their work already shows up in real-world AI use, on a score from 0 to 1.

Jobs built around text, data and code are the most exposed. Computer programmers lead with a score of 0.75, followed by customer service representatives and data entry keyers. These are mostly office jobs, which raises the next question: where in the world are these jobs?

Source: Anthropic Economic Index, job exposure (US occupations).

How does exposure differ between countries?

Here each country's workforce is split by its level of exposure to generative AI, from jobs that are not exposed to the most exposed (gradient 4).

    Exposure follows the type of economy. In Switzerland, the United Kingdom and France, roughly one worker in six holds a highly exposed job (gradients 3 and 4). In Nigeria and Bangladesh it is fewer than one in fifty, as far more people work in farming and manual jobs there. But even inside one country, workers are not equally exposed.

    Source: ILOSTAT, employment by sex and generative AI exposure, latest available year for each country.

    Are women and men equally exposed?

    Taking France as an example, each square below is 1% of employed women or men.

      No. About 18% of employed women in France work in highly exposed jobs, compared with about 11% of men. In the most exposed group, gradient 4, women are almost three times as likely to work as men (6% against 2%), since many of these are clerical and administrative jobs.

      Source: ILOSTAT, employment by sex and generative AI exposure, France, 2025.
