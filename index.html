AI and the Future of Employment

Who is most exposed, and how is work changing?

Introduction

Artificial intelligence is moving quickly from research labs into everyday work. This website asks one main question: how does the rapid development and adoption of AI affect the transformation of the global labor market?

We answer it in sections, each looking at the data from a different angle. The first section compares how AI reaches different jobs, countries, people and businesses. Later sections will add other views.

The website is for students, employees, employers, policymakers, and anyone interested in understanding how AI is transforming employment and the future of work.

Comparing AI Exposure and Adoption

How do jobs, countries, people and businesses compare in their exposure to AI and their use of it?

AI does not affect every worker in the same way. To answer this, we look at five smaller questions, starting with individual jobs and widening the view to countries, people and businesses.

On a small screen, swipe a chart sideways to see all of it.

Which jobs are most exposed to AI?

A good place to start is the work itself. This chart ranks professions by how much of their work already shows up in real-world AI use, on a score from 0 to 1.

Jobs built around text, data and code are the most exposed. Computer programmers lead with a score of 0.75, followed by customer service representatives and data entry keyers. These are mostly office jobs, which raises the next question: where in the world are these jobs?

Source: Anthropic Economic Index, job exposure (US occupations).

How does exposure differ between countries?

Here each country's workforce is split by its level of exposure to generative AI, from jobs that are not exposed to the most exposed (gradient 4).

    Exposure follows the type of economy. In Switzerland, the United Kingdom and France, roughly one worker in six holds a highly exposed job (gradients 3 and 4). In Nigeria and Bangladesh it is fewer than one in fifty, as far more people work in farming and manual jobs there. But even inside one country, workers are not equally exposed.

    Source: ILOSTAT, employment by sex and generative AI exposure, latest available year for each country.

    Are women and men equally exposed?

    Taking France as an example, each square below is 1% of employed women or men.

      No. About 18% of employed women in France work in highly exposed jobs, compared with about 11% of men. In the most exposed group, gradient 4, women are almost three times as likely to work as men (6% against 2%), since many of these are clerical and administrative jobs. So far we have seen where AI could change work. Next: are businesses actually using it?

      Source: ILOSTAT, employment by sex and generative AI exposure, France, 2025.

      How fast are businesses adopting AI?

      This heatmap shows the share of enterprises using at least one AI technology in each European country, from 2021 to 2025.

        Very fast, and especially since 2023. Across the EU, the share of enterprises using AI went from about 8% in 2023 to 20% in 2025. The gap between countries is still wide: 42% of enterprises in Denmark use AI, but only 5% in Romania.

        Source: Eurostat, artificial intelligence in enterprises (isoc_eb_ai), enterprises with 10 or more employees. No data was collected in 2022.

        Are large companies adopting AI faster than small ones?

        Finally, the same 2025 adoption data split by company size.

          Yes, in every country. Across the EU, 55% of large companies use AI, compared with 17% of small ones. The difference is biggest where adoption is low: in Poland large companies are about eight times as likely as small ones to use AI, in Denmark about twice.

          Source: Eurostat, artificial intelligence in enterprises (isoc_eb_ai), 2025.

          What does this mean for the labor market?

          Back to our main question. AI's effect on the labor market is real but uneven. It reaches office jobs built around text, data and code first, so it matters most in richer economies, and, at least in France, more for women than for men. Businesses are adopting it quickly, with large companies leading.

          These charts show where AI could change work, not how many jobs have been lost. Being exposed means AI can do or support part of a job, which may change that job rather than replace it.
